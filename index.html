US Constitution

You are graded on

  • Recall the branch of government that establishes military authority.
  • List the three US Constitution governing principles.
  • Recall the Amendments to the US Constitution that govern military officer authority.

1. Branch that establishes military authority

  • Executive (Art. II, §2): the President is Commander in Chief and commissions every officer. Your authority runs Constitution → presidential commission → your oath → you.
  • Legislative (Art. I, §8): Congress declares war, raises and maintains armies and a navy, and funds them.

2. Three governing principles

PrincipleMeaning
Enumerated powersGovernment has only the powers the Constitution explicitly grants.
Separation of powersLegislative, executive, and judicial branches split power and check each other.
FederalismPower is divided and shared between national and state governments (Tenth Amendment).

Professional and Unprofessional Relationships

You are graded on

  • Describe professional and unprofessional relationships.
  • Summarize the policy on professional and unprofessional relationships.
  • Summarize the general and specific prohibitions relating to unprofessional relationships for officers.
  • Summarize the effects of unprofessional relationships on unit cohesiveness.

1. Professional vs. unprofessional

  • Professional: meets expected standards of conduct. Mentoring, unit events, intramurals, and community projects are encouraged because they build cohesion.
  • Unprofessional (on or off duty) if it compromises, or appears to compromise, the chain of command; causes actual or perceived favoritism; misuses grade or position for gain; is exploitative or coercive; or harms good order, discipline, morale, or the mission. A relationship can become unprofessional when circumstances change.

2. Policy

Members of different grades keep a professional relationship built on mutual respect, dignity, and military courtesy. Personal relationships are individual choices until they adversely affect (or reasonably could affect) morale, good order, discipline, respect for authority, unit cohesion, or the mission.

Recruiter/trainer rule: prohibitions begin at first contact and end six months after accession and initial skills training, or at completion of initial entry training at the first duty assignment, whichever is later.

3. Officer prohibitions

General (UCMJ exposure)Specific (officer with enlisted)
Fraternization (Art. 134): applies across chains, services, and components. The officer alone is liable; the enlisted member can still face Art. 92.
Failure to obey an order
Dereliction of duty
Conduct unbecoming		 No gambling.
No lending, borrowing, or debt (exception: infrequent, small, non-interest loans for exigent circumstances).
No dating or sex.
No shared living space (unless required for a military operation).
No business ventures or solicited sales.

Marriage: a later marriage does not erase earlier fraternization, and an officer–enlisted marriage can itself be fraternization if it compromises the chain of command or good order.

4. Effects on cohesion

Even the appearance of a boss–buddy relationship erodes morale, respect for authority, and discipline, and undermines other leaders holding standards. Team-building activities are fine; avoid undue familiarity.

Critical Thinking

You are graded on

  • Summarize the definition of critical thinking.
  • Discuss methods for evaluating information.
  • Explain the nine intellectual standards used in assessing one’s thinking.
  • Explain cognitive domain operations and their relationship to disinformation.
  • Describe the elements of reasoning that are used in the critical thinking process.
  • Discuss common thinking errors that can lead to faulty thinking.

1. Definition

Thinking carefully about a subject or idea without allowing feelings or opinions to affect you.

2. Evaluating information

  • Fact = provable, verifiable. Opinion = subjective belief. Objective claims are true or false regardless of belief; subjective claims depend on what people think.
  • Ask what was left out. Confirmation bias (seeking what confirms you): “bend your beliefs to fit the facts.” Also recency bias and social proofing (following the group).

3. Nine intellectual standards

StandardTest
ClarityGateway: if unclear, you can’t judge the rest.
AccuracyTrue to reality? Verifiable?
PrecisionExact enough? (“Hot” vs. the temperature)
RelevanceBears on the issue?
DepthComplexities and assumptions addressed?
BreadthOther viewpoints considered?
LogicDo the pieces fit together?
SignificanceWhat matters most?
Fairness“Gut check”: is ego or self-interest distorting this?

4. Cognitive domain operations and disinformation

They blend information, psychological, and cyber warfare, aided by AI, to spread propaganda across the information domain. Disinformation is the delivery mechanism. Defense: expect incomplete or missing information, ask for clear information, and don’t act on half-truths or emotionally charged claims.

5. Eight elements of reasoning

ElementKey idea
PurposeIs it in line with my goals and values? Beware false purposes.
QuestionMost important; defines the problem (fact, preference, judgment).
InformationBeware activated ignorance (false info used as truth).
ConceptMost powerful; beware conceptual traps.
InferenceConclusion drawn from assumptions; don’t jump to conclusions.
AssumptionValue-based or descriptive; make the subconscious conscious.
Point of viewKnow your own, then enter others’.
ImplicationsWhat you expect if you decide this way.

6. Common thinking errors

ErrorLooks like
System 1 too fastFast, intuitive, emotional. Use slow, deliberate System 2 for consequential decisions.
Wrong questionsLeading questions that protect your view.
StereotypesSimplified ideas about groups.
Halo effectOne known trait colors everything else.
Belief perseveranceWon’t change your mind even when proven wrong.
DeflectionAnswering with something that doesn’t answer the question.

Organizational Climate

You are graded on

  • Contrast the difference between climate and culture.
  • Discuss strategies to build a positive organizational climate.
  • Describe how to positively change an organizational climate.

1. Climate vs. culture

ClimateCulture
How people feel right now: morale, perceptions. Changeable; measured by DEOCS.Deep, long-standing shared values and “how we do things here.” Slow to change.

2. Building a positive climate (Goldfein)

Over 95% of Airmen arrive intending to succeed, so remove barriers: make the environment safe, secure, and of high standards.

  • Physical safety: hazards controlled, risks managed.
  • Emotional safety: respect and openness; freedom to try new things without fear of harsh repercussions. Harassment and assault: zero tolerance.
  • High standards: define them by setting expectations tied to the mission.

3. Changing the climate: three moves

  1. Address your unit. Give a transparent DEOCS out-brief: positive and negative results, trends, mission impact, strategies, and follow-up services.
  2. Inspire action. Communicate mission, vision, and priorities often; enable action; reinforce with appreciation.
  3. Focus on the little things. You influence the unit by what you highlight, reward, reprimand, and even casually comment on.

Source note: The reader never defines “culture” explicitly. The contrast uses the standard distinction. Use the reader’s DEOCS language for climate.

Inspiring Action

You are graded on

  • Recognize the difference between mission and vision statements.
  • Recall the purpose of using the SWOT method to identify priorities.
  • Describe techniques for encouraging continued action toward a vision.

1. Mission vs. vision

MissionVision
What the organization does.Why it matters (Sinek); the aspiration and future state.

A good vision is idealistic yet achievable; articulated well and easily understood; gives clarity of purpose and direction; shared and supported.

2. Purpose of SWOT

Reveals positive forces that work together and potential problems to recognize and address, so priorities rest on facts, not assumptions. Internal: Strengths, Weaknesses. External: Opportunities, Threats.

Strategy matrix: OS use strengths to seize opportunities · OW overcome weaknesses using opportunities · TS use strengths to avoid threats · TW minimize weaknesses and avoid threats.

3. Keeping action moving toward the vision

  • Vision fades unless attended to, nourished, reviewed, and instilled.
  • Build it into meetings, decisions, priorities, and performance appraisals; communicate often by modeling, sharing, and inspiring.
  • Collaborate and accept feedback; followers help by modeling, sharing, and supporting it.

Military Justice

You are graded on

  • Identify the functions of the Uniform Code of Military Justice (UCMJ) and Manual for Courts-Martial (MCM).
  • Recognize the need for a military justice system.
  • List service members’ rights within the military justice system.
  • Identify the methods and associated tools available within the UCMJ to maintain discipline.
  • Recall the functions of the Staff Judge Advocate, Area Defense Counsel, and the Victims Counsel.

1. UCMJ and MCM

Congress enacted the UCMJ (5 May 1950) to unify and replace the separate Army, Navy, and Coast Guard disciplinary codes. The MCM (President Truman, executive order, 8 Feb 1951) is the President’s implementing manual: it has the force of law, contains the Constitution, the UCMJ, court-martial procedure, rules of evidence, and the punitive articles, and is designed so it rarely needs separate reference back to the UCMJ.

2. Why a separate system

  • Crimes unique to the military (e.g., AWOL) are not crimes in civilian life but can endanger a mission.
  • Familiarity: military members better understand how an offense (e.g., dereliction) endangers the mission and are more qualified to sit on courts trying it.
  • Worldwide jurisdiction: the Constitution doesn’t extend US civilian courts overseas, but the UCMJ/MCM apply wherever forces are stationed or deployed.

3. Service members’ rights

  • Article 31 rights against self-incrimination (the military’s equivalent of Miranda); a statement taken without advisement can’t be used at court-martial.
  • Right to counsel: a free Area Defense Counsel (ADC) before summary, special, and general courts-martial and before accepting Article 15 punishment; civilian counsel is allowed at the member’s own expense.
  • Search and seizure protections: most searches/inspections need a commander’s or military judge’s authorization; health and welfare inspections need no probable cause but can’t be used as a pretext to gather evidence for a specific suspect; consent to a search must be voluntary, not mere acquiescence.

4. Tools to maintain discipline

TierTools
PreventiveCommunicating standards so members self-regulate; the preferred method.
AdministrativeCounseling → admonition → reprimand (LOC/LOA/LOR), control roster, Unfavorable Information File (UIF). Corrective, not punitive.
NonjudicialArticle 15: for offenses too serious for administrative tools but not serious enough for a court-martial conviction. Member may demand trial by court-martial instead.
JudicialSummary (enlisted only, minor offenses) → Special (intermediate severity) → General (most serious; can include death) courts-martial.

5. SJA, ADC, and VC

OfficeRepresents
Staff Judge Advocate (SJA)The commander and the Air/Space Force: prosecutes courts-martial, advises commanders and investigators, runs the base legal office (military justice, claims, civil law).
Area Defense Counsel (ADC)The accused member, with a chain of command kept separate from the SJA so neither the commander nor SJA can influence the defense.
Victims Counsel (VC)Victims of alleged sexual assault or domestic violence, also on a separate chain from the SJA.

What Is War

You are graded on

  • Identify the three enduring truths that describe the fundamental nature of war.
  • Identify the two basic themes of war.
  • List the three factors that dominate war.
  • Differentiate between the traditional and irregular forms of warfare.

1. Three enduring truths

  1. War is an instrument of policy. It starts from a political motive and stays subordinate to it (e.g., the 1991 Gulf War stopped once Kuwait was liberated). Effects are directed through the strategic (why/with what), operational (what, in what order), and tactical (how) levels.
  2. War is a complex, chaotic human endeavor. Friction turns simple actions into difficult ones.
  3. War is a clash of opposing wills. The enemy is a living, adaptive opponent, not an inanimate mass.

2. Two basic themes: Policy and Violence

PolicyViolence
The "will" a nation-state seeks to impose on their opponent.Raw, physical essence of war. The "act" itself. The application of force.

3. Three factors that dominate war

FactorMeaningExample
FogImperfect knowledge of what is happening.Allied overconfidence before the Battle of the Bulge.
FrictionCountless small things accumulating to force plans awry.Mud and logistics breakdowns slowing Operation Barbarossa.
ChancePure luck.The bomb blast deflected by a table leg in the July 1944 plot against Hitler.

4. Traditional vs. irregular warfare

TraditionalIrregular
A violent struggle for domination between nation-states, force-on-force, aiming to disarm the enemy’s military (e.g., Napoleon at Austerlitz).A violent struggle among state and non-state actors for legitimacy and influence, using indirect, asymmetric approaches to wear out a stronger enemy (e.g., the Soviet-Afghan War).

Not mutually exclusive: a single conflict can show both forms at once.

What the DAF Brings to the Fight

You are graded on

  • Recall the purpose of airpower and spacepower.
  • Recall the five USAF core functions and four USSF core and enterprise functions.
  • Describe the purpose of each USAF core function and USSF core and enterprise function.
  • Understand the role and purpose of doctrine to the USAF and USSF.

1. Purpose of airpower and spacepower

Airpower gives the joint force control of the air and applies precision strike, rapid global mobility, global ISR, and command and control so friendly forces have freedom of action across every domain. Spacepower gives the joint force a space-enabled combat edge: control of the space domain, space-based effects (communications, navigation, missile warning), and the means to move into, through, and back from space.

3. What each function does

  • Air Superiority: control of the air, from parity to supremacy, via offensive and defensive counterair.
  • Global Precision Attack: hold targets at risk rapidly and persistently (strategic attack, nuclear operations, air interdiction, CAS).
  • Rapid Global Mobility: move and sustain forces worldwide (airlift, air refueling, aeromedical evacuation).
  • Global ISR: synchronizes sensors and analysis to feed the joint intelligence process and targeting.
  • Command and Control (USAF): the central joint function that integrates all the others; includes mission command and battle management.
  • Space Control: contest and control the space domain to reach space superiority.
  • Global Mission Operations: deliver space effects (satcom, navigation warfare, missile warning) to the fight.
  • Space Access: move personnel and equipment into, through, and back from the space domain.

4. Role and purpose of doctrine

Doctrine is not a binding order like an instruction; it codifies what the Service has learned in practice into fundamental, officially sanctioned principles that guide — rather than dictate — how air and space forces are best employed to fight and win.

Military Planning Fundamentals

You are graded on

  • Explain why the military uses a formal planning process.
  • Summarize the seven steps of the Joint Planning Process (JPP).
  • Describe the attributes of a quality course of action (COA).
  • Summarize the different methods used to evaluate COAs.
  • Recall the purpose of wargaming.

1. Why a formal process

Operation EAGLE CLAW (1980) shows the cost of skipping one: an overly complex plan, insufficient rehearsals, weak weather contingencies, no redundancy, and poor interservice coordination led to eight deaths and an aborted rescue. A structured process can’t guarantee success, but it minimizes risk and forces a realistic assessment of challenges before committing forces.

2. The seven JPP steps

#StepProduces
1Planning InitiationA clear problem statement and a planning team.
2Mission AnalysisEssential tasks, constraints/restraints, facts/assumptions, COG analysis, CCIRs, and a Restated Mission Statement.
3COA DevelopmentMultiple suitable, feasible, acceptable, distinguishable, complete COAs.
4COA Analysis and WargamingEach COA’s strengths and weaknesses against a thinking adversary.
5COA ComparisonA ranked recommendation using an evaluation matrix.
6COA ApprovalThe commander’s decision and Commander’s Estimate.
7Plan or Order DevelopmentThe CONOPS, refined into the OPORD.

3. Attributes of a quality COA (“SFADC”)

  • Suitable – achieves the objective and commander’s intent.
  • Feasible – executable within available time, space, and resources.
  • Acceptable – benefit is worth the risk and cost.
  • Distinguishable – meaningfully different from the other COAs.
  • Complete – covers objectives, tasks, timing, and success criteria.

4. Methods used to evaluate COAs

Wargaming: Seminar, Matrix, Computer Assisted, Red Teaming

5. Purpose of wargaming

Wargaming gives the commander and staff a shared, tested understanding of the operational environment by simulating the human decision-making, fog, and friction of real conflict against a thinking enemy — not just a plan that looks good on paper. If it exposes a flaw, the team revises the COA and wargames it again.
